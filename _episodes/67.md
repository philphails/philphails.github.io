There is a quiet moment most people who ride long enough eventually reach. It is not the finish line or the personal best. It is the recognition that the bike has been doing something deeper than training the legs. It has been rearranging how you see yourself, how you meet difficulty, and how you move through the places you live.

That was the current running through my conversation with David Comer, president of Violet Crown Sports Association. David is an Austin native, a former UT cyclist, and someone who has spent the better part of a decade helping shape one of the city’s most enduring cycling communities. What stayed with me was not the race results or the club logistics. It was the way he talked about the body he once did not trust, and the slow work of learning to inhabit it again through miles shared with other people.

## From Unsteady Ground to Steady Wheels

David did not arrive at cycling as a natural athlete. He describes himself as a nerd who spent high school buried in rigorous academics. He had a period of significant weight gain, then a Crohn’s diagnosis that dropped him nearly ninety pounds in a year. The body he lived in felt unreliable. Fencing offered some early lessons in focus and timing, but it was the bike that eventually gave him a different kind of language for effort and recovery.

In college at UT, commuting became exploring. Short rides with roommates turned into longer ones. The Friday “Shootout” rides outside Gregory Gym mixed casual riders with people who had already completed long tours. The pace was accessible most of the way, then opened up for short efforts. It was structured enough to be useful and open enough to feel welcoming. From there the distances grew—thirty miles, then forty, then the Violet Crown Sunday rides that stretched toward fifty and sixty.

What mattered was not the numbers. It was the gradual realization that the body could be asked for more and still respond. David credits those early group rides with the first sense that he might have athletic potential after years of not seeing himself that way. The identity shift was quiet and cumulative. It did not announce itself. It simply accumulated in the form of miles that no longer felt impossible.

## Community as Container, Not Scoreboard

One of the tensions that surfaces often in cycling is the hierarchy that forms on group rides. There is status in the newest bike, in the strongest legs, in the person who can hold the front for long stretches. David is clear-eyed about it. He also notes the quieter roles that keep a ride human—the storyteller, the joker, the person who makes the effort feel less solitary.

Violet Crown has managed to hold both high-performance racing and recreational riding under the same umbrella. That balance is harder than it sounds. The club has grown past 450 members, developed junior programs, and taken on events that feed the national pipeline. Yet the culture David describes still leans toward showing up, learning, and staying. He talks about the value of sitting near the back in a first race, of finding steady wheels, of treating the experience itself as the point rather than the result.

There is a practical wisdom here. Competition can sharpen focus. It can also shrink the circle until only the strongest feel they belong. A healthy community keeps the circle large enough that people can enter at different levels of fitness and still find a place. The long-term benefit is not only more riders. It is more people who learn that effort and belonging can coexist.

## Scale, Landscape, and the Body in Motion

David worked with problems of scale in his previous life as an environmental scientist. Cycling gives him another way to experience scale directly. On a bike you move slowly enough to notice the change in plants with altitude, the layers of rock, the way a storm can be watched from miles away before it arrives. The same mountain that looks abstract from a plane becomes something you inhabit for hours.

He tells stories of a cold, rainy century to Wimberley, of four days of bikepacking in Vermont where the route kept insisting the road was still passable, of a long climb up Mount Lemmon that ended in a storm and a freezing descent. None of these rides were perfect. Some ended short of the original plan. All of them left a clearer sense of what the body and mind can hold when the conditions are not ideal.

The invitation that emerges is simple and demanding at once: bite off a little more than you think you can chew. Not recklessly. Not as performance. As a way of discovering that the limits you assumed were fixed might be more flexible than they first appeared. The skill transfers. The same capacity that lets someone finish a hard day on the bike can show up later in work, in relationships, or in any domain where the next step feels larger than the current self.

## Personal Reflection

What stayed with me after talking with David was the absence of drama in how he described difficult things. Crohn’s, the sudden weight loss, the years of not seeing himself as athletic, the unexpected length of his time as club president—all of it was presented without the need to turn it into a triumphant arc. The bike did not “fix” him. It gave him a place to practice showing up when the body felt uncertain and the outcome was not guaranteed.

I recognized something familiar in that. Many of us carry earlier versions of ourselves that felt limited or unreliable. The work is rarely a single breakthrough. It is the accumulation of ordinary efforts that slowly rewrite the story we tell about what we can sustain. Riding with others makes that process less lonely. It also makes the lessons harder to ignore, because the feedback is immediate and shared.

I left the conversation thinking less about performance metrics and more about the quality of attention that long rides require. The pace of the bicycle forces a kind of presence that faster forms of travel do not. That presence is useful far beyond the road.

## Practical Takeaways

- Start where the body is, not where the ego wants it to be. Early rides that feel almost too easy can still build the foundation for harder efforts later.  
- Treat group rides as practice in belonging as much as fitness. The people who make the effort feel human matter as much as the ones who set the pace.  
- When entering a new competitive space, sit near the back and learn the flow before trying to lead it. Observation is a form of preparation.  
- Use longer rides to notice scale—landscape, weather, fatigue, recovery. The same awareness transfers to other areas of life where problems feel abstract until you move through them slowly.  
- Allow room for imperfect days. Some of the most lasting rides end short of the original plan and still expand what feels possible.  
- Look for communities that hold both high standards and wide doors. The combination is rarer than it should be and more valuable than pure performance culture.

## Conclusion

Cycling will not solve every question of identity or resilience. It does offer a concrete place to practice the same capacities those questions require: patience with the body, attention to what is actually happening, and the willingness to keep moving when the conditions are less than ideal. David Comer’s path—from an uncertain body to a role of steady leadership within a large cycling community—suggests that the most useful growth often arrives through ordinary miles shared with other people rather than through dramatic single moments.

The next ride does not need to be epic. It only needs to be honest about where you are starting and open to the possibility that the body and the mind can still surprise you.

## Guest Bio

David Comer serves as the current president of the Violet Crown Sports Association. He joined in 2017 as a member of the men's racing team and assumed the presidency in 2020 after serving on the Board for two years. Under his leadership, the Club has grown to over 450 members, established programs to connect children with the sport through cyclocross and road racing, and has acquired and developed the La Primavera Lago Vista bicycle race, turning it into a premier regional event for U18 racers and a pipeline for the US National team.

Outside of Violet Crown, David runs the Camp of Lamps, an events company that produces fan-favorite road and cyclocross races  and provides support for clients such as the Rosedale Ride and the Black History Bike Ride. You can ride with David on Violet Crown's Tuesday and Thursday morning rides, or catch him on the weekends at local road or cyclocross events.
