Some people discover their purpose in a single dramatic moment. Most of us find it the long way—through repetition, imperfect gigs, late nights, and the slow recognition that certain work makes us feel most like ourselves.

In this conversation with DJ Mel, we explored that longer arc. Mel has spent decades inside the music world as a DJ, a student of the craft, and someone who keeps showing up even when the pay is low or the room is small. What emerged was less a success story and more a living example of how purpose can grow out of sustained attention to the thing you love.

The episode carried the familiar Phil Phails framing—this time around finding purpose, niche, or mission. Mel’s answers were practical, grounded, and free of slogans.

![Practice Over Performance](/episodes/assets/images/practice-over-performance.jpg)

## The Practice of Showing Up

One of the clearest threads in the conversation was Mel’s relationship to gigs. He described taking a date for less money than he has received in a long time simply because he liked the person booking him and because he wanted to DJ. The decision was not framed as sacrifice. It was framed as staying in contact with the work itself.

He returns often to the idea of practice. A wrong note or a bad transition is not a reason to stop. You roll with it and keep going. Technical difficulties during our own recording became an accidental illustration of the same point. The conversation continued. The craft continued.

That stance—show up, play, treat the set as practice—runs through decades of Mel’s experience. It is not romanticized. It is simply what he has found works.

## Music as Identity and History

Mel’s relationship with music is both personal and archival. He talks easily about first records, early record stores, the albums that marked different chapters of his life, and the musicians who shaped his ear. His own podcast, *The Sound of First*, extends that interest outward—conversations about first concerts, first record stores, and the songs that marked personal turning points become a way of mapping how music enters and rearranges a life.

During our conversation I found myself slipping into familiar habits: naming bands during workouts, referencing Coltrane, Freddie Hubbard, and the years I spent studying jazz at the University of North Texas. Those details belong to my own long relationship with sound. What stood out with Mel was how naturally he met that same language. Music is not a side interest for him. It is the organizing principle.

## Work, Balance, and the Therapist

The conversation also touched the cost side of deep engagement. Mel has worked with a therapist around workaholism. The same drive that made him reliable and skilled also required conscious attention. We did not fully unpack the balance question in this sitting—there is clear appetite on both sides to return to it—but the acknowledgment itself mattered. Purpose without some form of regulation can become another kind of exhaustion.

## Personal Reflection

What stayed with me was the absence of performance around the idea of purpose. Mel did not present a polished origin story or a five-step method. He described a long series of choices to keep playing, keep learning, and keep treating the work as something worth showing up for even when the conditions were imperfect.

I recognized the tension many of us carry: the desire for a clear mission alongside the reality that clarity often arrives only after years of imperfect practice. Mel’s example suggests that the practice itself can be the path rather than a temporary phase on the way to something more official.

![Practical Takeaways](/episodes/assets/images/71-practical-takeaways.jpg)

## Practical Takeaways

- Treat ordinary gigs and imperfect sets as legitimate practice rather than merely stepping stones.  
- When the work itself still feels true, low pay or small rooms do not automatically disqualify the opportunity.  
- Technical mistakes and difficult transitions are part of the craft; the useful skill is continuing.  
- A long relationship with music—first records, formative albums, ongoing listening—can become a reliable source of identity and orientation.  
- Deep engagement with any craft often requires some form of conscious balance work. Therapy can be part of that.  
- Purpose is frequently discovered in the sustained doing rather than declared in advance.

## Conclusion

DJ Mel has spent a lifetime inside the music. The conversation did not resolve into a neat statement of mission. It offered something more useful: a working example of someone who keeps choosing the craft, keeps practicing in public, and keeps finding himself in the act of playing.

The cameras eventually gave out. The conversation did not feel finished. That, too, felt consistent with the subject. Some relationships with the work are not meant to be concluded in a single sitting. They are meant to be continued.

## Guest Bio

**DJ Mel**, widely known as “Obama’s DJ,” is one of Austin’s most accomplished and enduring DJs. Over a career spanning more than three decades, he has performed at major festivals including Lollapalooza, Austin City Limits, Bonnaroo, and Voodoo, as well as high-profile events such as Super Bowl LX, Formula 1 United States Grand Prix, the X Games, and multiple NFL Drafts.

His political résumé is equally notable. Mel served as DJ for Barack Obama’s 57th Presidential Inauguration, the 2012 Democratic National Convention, Election Night 2012 at Obama Headquarters, Obama’s Farewell Address, and the White House Easter Egg Roll across multiple years. He has also supported campaigns and events for Bernie Sanders, Beto O’Rourke, and James Talarico.

A cornerstone of the Austin music scene, Mel ran one of the longest-running weekly DJ residencies in the United States at Nasty’s from 1996 to 2017. He currently serves as the Official DJ for Texas Longhorns Women’s Basketball and previously held the same role for the Men’s team from 2015 to 2025. In 2014 he was inducted into the Austin Music Hall of Fame, and in 2019 the City of Austin proclaimed June 6 as DJ Mel Day.

He continues to tour nationally and internationally while remaining deeply rooted in Austin.
