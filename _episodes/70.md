There’s a particular kind of belonging that happens on a group ride. You show up with your bike, your legs, and whatever version of yourself the week has left you with. Then the wheels start turning and, for a while, the rest of it falls away.

In this conversation with Jay Bond—Oliver James Bond V, if we’re being formal—we explored the living culture of Violet Crown, one of Austin’s longstanding recreational cycling groups. What began as a local ride has become something larger: a portable community, a weekly ritual, and a quiet archive of the people who keep showing up.

Jay has been part of that world for years. He carries family history that stretches back to the Citadel in South Carolina, a dry sense of humor about his own name, and a deep, unflashy love of the ride itself. This episode is the first in what will hopefully become a longer series looking at Austin cycling and the broader American cycling landscape through the people who keep it alive.

## The Name and the Place

Jay’s full name is Oliver James Bond V. The Roman numeral gets attention. So does the James Bond association. He takes both in stride. His great-grandfather served as president of the Citadel; the administration building there still carries the family name. Yet Jay describes both sides of his family as essentially poor. The name suggests something the bank account never quite delivered.

That contrast—grand name, ordinary means—feels oddly fitting for a conversation about recreational cycling. The sport has its own hierarchies and status symbols, but the weekly group ride tends to level them. You either show up and pedal or you don’t.

## Why Violet Crown Matters

Violet Crown is more than a route or a schedule. It’s a container for people who might otherwise never meet. On any given Sunday you can find locals, visitors from Europe or Australia, people who work in oil, law, tech, or something entirely different. Jay has handed a spare jersey to a rider from the suburbs of Amsterdam. Friends have spotted Violet Crown kits in Belgium. The group travels farther than most of its members realize.

![Belonging on the Ride](/episodes/assets/images/belonging-on-the-ride.jpg)

Jay is clear-eyed about the practical side. He spent years as a state employee and now lives on a pension and Social Security. He understands that love alone doesn’t pay the mortgage. At the same time, he keeps showing up for the rides because the rides give him something the rest of the week rarely does: uncomplicated presence with other people moving in the same direction.

## Mentoring the Series

One of the quiet gifts of the conversation was Jay’s willingness to mentor the project itself. He pushed for a wider frame—Austin cycling and American cycling more broadly—rather than a narrow focus on one club. He offered names, connections, and practical advice about building an audience. The episode ends with the sense that this is only part one.

That generosity feels characteristic of the best group-ride culture. People pass on knowledge, routes, gear recommendations, and sometimes a jersey that doesn’t fit them anymore. The transaction is rarely formal. It just happens because someone decided the ride was better when more people could join it.

## Personal Reflection

What stayed with me is how ordinary the belonging is. No grand mission statement. No heavy ideology. Just a recurring invitation to meet at a certain time, ride a certain distance, and share the road for a while.

I’ve spent a lot of time thinking about formal containers for growth—men’s groups, recovery rooms, structured practices. Violet Crown offers a different model: show up, move your body, talk if you want to, and let the miles do some of the work. It doesn’t solve everything. It doesn’t claim to. But it keeps a certain kind of human connection available in a city that can feel increasingly transactional.

Jay’s presence in the conversation was steady and unpretentious. He knows the history, the people, and the quiet jokes that hold a group together. Listening to him, I was reminded that some of the most durable communities are the ones that never try too hard to be important.

![Practical Takeaways Jay Bond](/episodes/assets/images/practical-takeaways-jay-bond.jpg)

## Practical Takeaways

- Local group rides can function as low-pressure containers for belonging and conversation.  
- The culture of a ride is shaped as much by who keeps showing up as by the official route or rules.  
- Generosity on the bike—sharing knowledge, gear, or simple presence—tends to travel farther than we expect.  
- Broadening the frame of a story (from one club to a wider cycling culture) often makes the story more useful to more people.  
- Consistent, ordinary participation can be a form of quiet leadership.  
- Paying attention to the people who have been around the longest is usually a good way to understand a community’s real history.

## Conclusion

Violet Crown is one chapter in a larger story about how people find each other on two wheels. Jay Bond has lived inside that chapter long enough to know its textures—the jokes, the hierarchies, the unexpected friendships, and the simple pleasure of a well-paced group ride on a Sunday morning.

This conversation is only the beginning. There are more voices, more routes, and more history still to gather. For now, it is enough to say that the ride continues, and that showing up still matters.

## Guest Bio

**Jay Bond** (Oliver James Bond V) is a longtime Austin cyclist and active member of the Violet Crown riding community. With deep family roots in South Carolina—including a great-grandfather who served as president of the Citadel—Jay brings both historical perspective and grounded humor to conversations about cycling culture, group rides, and the quiet ways people build belonging on the bike. He has ridden extensively in the Austin area and remains a steady presence in the local recreational cycling scene.
