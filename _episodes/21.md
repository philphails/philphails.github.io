There’s a quiet tension most of us carry. We know we should slow down. We know the best ideas arrive when we stop forcing them. Yet the culture around us rewards motion—more output, more plans, more noise.  

In this conversation with James McCrae—author, teacher, and self-described meme poet—we explored what it means to cultivate intuition in a world that rarely makes space for it. James frames intuition not as a mysterious gift reserved for a few, but as a practice of listening. The failure, he suggests, is not in lacking intuition. The failure is in drowning it out.

We began with one of his poems, “Sing Along,” a piece that asks when we last gave ourselves permission to play without agenda. It set the tone for everything that followed: a return to the inner child, a rejection of the well-dressed corpse of busyness, and an invitation to treat life as a sandbox rather than a performance.

## How We Fail at Cultivating Intuition

James’s answer to the question of failure was simple and precise. We fail by thinking too much and by staying in constant motion.  

Intuition, he said, is the art of listening and receptivity. In Taoist terms, it is the yin side of the equation. Our culture leans hard into yang—action, productivity, analysis, control. When the mind is full of information and the calendar is full of tasks, there is no room left for inspiration.  

A mind that is too busy has no space for the quiet whisper that actually knows the way. The ego stays in overdrive, calculating and scheming, while the deeper guidance waits for stillness. Meditation, for James, is not an optional spiritual add-on. It is daily practice in getting out of the way so the muse can speak.

I recognized myself in that description. Many of us treat stillness as a luxury we will get to once everything else is finished. The irony is that the more we postpone listening, the more our actions become busy rather than purposeful.

## Success Looks Different for Everyone

When I asked what success in cultivating intuition might look like, James refused a single answer. There is no universal finish line. Everyone’s intuition points toward a different purpose.  

The danger, he noted, is adopting society’s definitions of success and then forcing ourselves into shapes that do not fit. We end up compromising the very vision our intuition is trying to protect. Real success, in this frame, is the willingness to trust your own path even when it does not match the external metrics. Failures become stepping stones rather than verdicts.

This lands close to the spirit of Phil Phails. The show itself is built on the idea that the space between starting and arriving is where the real learning happens. Intuition does not eliminate stumbles. It changes the relationship we have with them.

## Intuition and Expression as Yin and Yang

One of the clearest threads in the conversation was the relationship between inner listening and outer expression. For James, the two are inseparable. Intuition is the inner journey. Expression—whether through writing, art, podcasting, or memes—is the outer journey.  

They function as yin and yang. Without the receptive side, expression becomes hollow performance. Without the expressive side, intuition stays private and unused. A writing practice, in this view, is not primarily about producing content. It is a way of training the channel between the two.

James’s own path illustrates the point. He moved through poetry, graphic design, brand strategy, books, and eventually memes—each form a different way of letting the same inner material take shape in the world. His current work, including *The Art of You*, is an attempt to help others reclaim that same capacity.

## The Inner Child and the Creative Life Force

The poem that opened our conversation returned again and again. Childhood, James writes, is not an age. It is an attitude of curiosity and wonder. We do not outgrow the child inside. We bind it with social rules and turn up the volume of busyness so we cannot hear it cry.  

Creativity, in this light, is not a special talent. It is the natural expression of the life force that already moves through us. James is clear on this: we are all equally spiritual because we are all animated by spirit. The trees outside, the body we inhabit, the ideas that arrive unbidden—they participate in the same creative current.  

Learning to create is learning to let that current move through us rather than blocking it with fear, perfectionism, or the need for external approval. In a culture that often feels stuck in survival and decay, he sees a need for rebirth—a renaissance that begins with individuals reclaiming their capacity to imagine and then make something real.

## Personal Reflection

What stayed with me after talking with James is how ordinary the practice of intuition actually is. It does not require dramatic visions or special states. It requires the willingness to stop filling every silence and to notice what arrives when the noise drops.  

I have spent years treating productivity as the primary measure of a day well spent. The conversation left me more aware of the cost. The more I crowd the mental space, the less room there is for the quieter intelligence that actually knows what matters.  

I also left thinking about the inner child not as something to heal once and for all, but as a capacity that needs regular permission. Play without agenda. Dance for no reason. Create without immediately turning the result into content or currency. These are not indulgences. They are maintenance for the channel.

## Practical Takeaways

- Treat intuition as the art of listening rather than a mysterious talent you either have or lack.  
- Notice when busyness and overthinking are crowding out the quieter signals. Stillness is not the absence of action; it is the precondition for aligned action.  
- Use a simple daily practice—meditation, journaling, or even a few minutes of silence—to create space between thoughts.  
- Define success on your own terms instead of measuring yourself against external metrics that may not fit your actual purpose.  
- Hold intuition and expression as partners. Listen first, then let what you hear take form through whatever medium is natural to you.  
- Give the inner child regular permission to play without an agenda. Curiosity and wonder are not childish; they are creative fuel.  
- Remember that creativity is not reserved for special people. It is the same life force already moving through your body and the world around you.

## Conclusion

Cultivating intuition is less about adding a new skill and more about removing the interference. The guidance is already present. The question is whether we are quiet enough, and receptive enough, to hear it.  

James McCrae’s work—and this conversation—offers a simple and demanding invitation: make space. Listen. Then let what you hear move through you into form. In a culture that rarely stops, that practice itself becomes a quiet act of resistance and a seed for something new.

## Guest Bio

**James McCrae** is an author, poet, and teacher who empowers creators to live with purpose and turn imagination into reality. He is the founder of Sunflower Club, a global community dedicated to creativity as a tool for personal healing and social transformation. As a creative strategist, he has worked with top brands and startups to define and actualize their message and mission. His books include *Sh#t Your Ego Says*, *How to Laugh in Ironic Amusement During Your Existential Crisis*, and *The Art of You: The Essential Guidebook for Reclaiming Your Creativity*.
